Lead Story

Commerce closes the cloud loophole: licence now required for advanced-AI-chip access via data centres in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil — US Department of Commerce / Federal Register[1]

The US Bureau of Industry and Security's interim final rule on “Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors” was published in the Federal Register on Thursday 2026-11-19 and took effect the same day.[1] A new Section 744.24 of the Export Administration Regulations requires an export, re-export, or in-country transfer licence for Covered Items when the exporter has reason to know they will reach a data centre — anywhere in the world — that supplies more than 12 million aggregate Total Processing Performance to customers headquartered in China or other specified destinations.[1] On top of that, a parallel control requires licences for shipments of the covered chips to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil specifically.[1]

CNBC's reporting from the same week lays out why the five countries: Malaysia and Singapore were already the pressure points named by the Commerce investigation into the alleged Nvidia-H100 smuggling network, Thailand and Indonesia are where the Southeast-Asian data-centre build-out is accelerating fastest, and Brazil has become a non-trivial renter of frontier-grade compute to third parties.[2] Fortune confirmed Singapore and Malaysia were explicitly the targets of the new interim rule, flagging the scandal timing.[3] The carve-out matters as much as the rule: US-headquartered end-users and their subsidiaries do not need a licence, which means Microsoft, Google, Oracle and AWS can continue to buy and operate the chips through their regional subsidiaries, while independent Southeast-Asian data-centre operators and any Chinese tenant of theirs now cannot — the control is a structural preference for US-cloud operators abroad, not a geographic ban.[1]

The ninety-day window is the operative detail: facilities already serving large Chinese customers have until mid-February 2027 to apply for a licence or lose access to covered chips.[1] A sixty-day public-comment period runs through 2027-01-18, so the rule can be amended, but it is enforceable now, not pending finalisation.[1] For any product that depends on inference capacity in the five named countries, this is the week your capacity-planning model stops being a procurement spreadsheet and starts being a compliance one.

So what: if your production inference or batch workload runs in a Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia or Brazil data centre that is NOT a US-headquartered hyperscaler subsidiary, you have ninety days to either migrate to a US-cloud regional subsidiary, qualify for a licence, or accept that your chip supply chain is now one BIS action away from throttling — and your 2027 capacity forecast needs a named path, not a footnote.

New York AG stands up the ASSET Unit to enforce the RAISE Act from 1 January — Office of the New York Attorney General[4]

Attorney General Letitia James announced the AI Safety, Standards, Enforcement and Transparency (ASSET) Unit on 2026-11-17, with Cristy Phillips (previously Special Counsel for the Economic Justice Division) as lead. The unit sits inside the Economic Justice Division, works with the Bureau of Internet and Technology, and has exclusive authority to enforce the RAISE Act, under which any frontier AI developer with more than $500M annual revenue must register with New York by 2027-01-01 and report critical safety incidents within 72 hours.[4] It is the first dedicated AI-enforcement unit at a US state-AG office, and the whistleblower tip line went live with the announcement.[4]

So what: if you operate a frontier model and your revenue clears $500M, treat the January 1 registration as a hard deadline and rehearse your 72-hour incident-report runbook now — a RAISE Act “critical safety incident” will land on the ASSET Unit's desk, not a consumer-protection generalist, and “we were still drafting the incident classification” is not a 72-hour answer.
