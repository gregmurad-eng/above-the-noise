The EU AI Act's Enforcement Era Begins — and the First Fines Are Coming

On August 2, 2026, the theoretical era of AI compliance ended. The core obligations of the European Union's AI Act — long treated by many US-based labs and product teams as a distant hypothetical — moved from roadmap into enforceable law. Regulators now have the authority to levy fines of up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, against companies that violate the rules. That ceiling puts the AI Act among the most consequential technology statutes ever passed, exceeding even the maximum penalties available under the GDPR.

The obligations biting first are the transparency rules for general-purpose AI models and the prohibitions on the highest-risk uses — social scoring, real-time biometric identification in public spaces, and emotion recognition in schools and workplaces. Providers of frontier models now have documented duties to publish training-data summaries, honor copyright reservations, and cooperate with the newly-empowered AI Office in Brussels. High-risk system rules — including credit scoring, insurance pricing, hiring, and critical-infrastructure use cases — remain on a staggered timeline, with stand-alone systems due into force December 2, 2027 and embedded-in-product systems on August 2, 2028.

The market response has been notable for what it isn't: panic. Most major labs quietly published or updated their model cards, training-data disclosures, and copyright-opt-out mechanisms in the weeks leading up to August 2. What's changed is the enforcement posture. Member-state regulators have telegraphed that the first enforcement actions — expected within 60 to 90 days — will target clear-cut prohibited-use violations rather than ambiguous transparency lapses, giving the industry a soft-landing runway on the harder questions.

Analysis: For US builders, the second-order effect matters more than the fines. The AI Act's transparency and provenance requirements are becoming a de-facto global baseline — not because Brussels has extraterritorial reach, but because the cheapest engineering path is to build one compliant pipeline and ship it everywhere. Expect the model-card and training-data-summary formats codified by the AI Office to quietly become industry conventions by year-end, the same way GDPR's cookie banners and DSAR flows became global infrastructure. The regulatory arbitrage window is closing. Teams still betting on "we'll deal with it if we get big" are underwriting a debt they don't fully understand.
