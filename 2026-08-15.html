Lead Story

— Z.ai / Bloomberg / SiliconANGLE

GLM-5.3 tops the open-weights coding board on post-training alone — and finds real vulnerabilities Z.ai didn't train it to find

On August 14 Z.ai shipped GLM-5.3, an update to its 743-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts base model. The headline claim is unusual: nothing about the base changed. "Scaling post-training is all we did for GLM-5.3," the company wrote in its release announcement.[1] Every capability gain — and there are big ones — came from a longer, more expensive post-training run on the same weights that shipped as GLM-5.2 in June.[2]

The coding numbers are the loud part. On Terminal-Bench 3.0, which measures long-horizon command-line agentic work, GLM-5.3 jumped from 4.6 to 28.3 and now leads all open-weights systems the benchmark tracks.[4] On DeepSWE v1.1 the score moved from 46.2 to 66.9. Z.ai's internal coding benchmark showed a 50% improvement over GLM-5.2 at half the token budget of Claude Opus 4.8.[3] Bloomberg framed the release as Z.ai's play to close the coding gap on Anthropic's Fable 5 and OpenAI's frontier tier;[2] SiliconANGLE noted the model is now available through Z.ai's API and coding plan, with open weights promised on Hugging Face in roughly two weeks after safety hardening completes.[3]

The quieter part is more interesting. GLM-5.3 hit 84.5% on CyberGym — first place ahead of Mythos 5 (83.8%) and GPT-5.6 Sol (83.6%) — and more than doubled its ExploitBench score, from 24.4% to 54.4%.[4] Z.ai's own writeup says the vulnerability-discovery training scaled beyond what they planned: the model started chaining single-bug reasoning into multi-step exploitation on its own. During evaluation it found more than 2,400 vulnerabilities across 269 open-source projects, roughly half rated medium severity or above, including bugs in code written 40 years ago.[1] A capability the lab did not fully anticipate emerged from post-training scale alone, on an unchanged base.

Two structural signals for builders. First, the "pretraining is where the capability lives" mental model is breaking down at the edges — post-training now moves the coding leaderboard by 24 points on a single benchmark. Second, cyber capability is no longer separable from coding capability: the same run that made GLM-5.3 the best open-weights coder also made it the best open-weights vulnerability finder, and Z.ai flagged the offensive-security edge as a scaling surprise, not a design target. When the weights drop in two weeks under the MIT license Z.ai has used for prior GLM releases, both capabilities ship to whoever downloads them.

If your coding-agent stack is built on Claude or GPT because "open weights aren't there yet," put a two-week hold on that assumption and re-run your eval suite against GLM-5.3 the day the weights drop. If your security team blocks self-hosted open-weights coders on capability-risk grounds, GLM-5.3 is the exact model to write the policy against — its vulnerability-discovery capability is now on the record.

Voices

Dr. Fei-Fei Li, Stanford computer science professor and co-founder of World Labs, on the Huberman Lab podcast released August 11, 2026 (episode: Using AI to Increase Your Intelligence & Enrich Humanity, ~3 hours):[12]

"The absolute bad outcome is that our young generation, their agency and human-level motivation of learning and living is taken away by tools."

Li argues the primary danger of AI in classrooms is not academic dishonesty but the erosion of intrinsic motivation — students losing the reason to learn at all — and that outright bans are equally corrosive. Her position is a targeted-integration one: use AI when a motivated learner is already stuck on a specific problem, not as a shortcut around the effort that produces understanding.[13] The framing generalises past classrooms. Any tool that mediates between a professional and the problem they are trying to solve can strip the same motivation if the friction it removes was the friction that produced the skill. Worth a full listen for the section on "agentic curiosity" and where in a workflow to place — and withhold — an assistant.

Before your next agentic feature ships, name the specific skill your user develops by struggling with the problem this feature removes. If the answer is "none," the feature is fine. If the answer is a load-bearing professional skill, you are not building a copilot — you are building the reason your users stop developing.

Today's Signal

The compute-side story this week is that base pretraining is no longer where the marginal gains live. GLM-5.3 took the open-weights coding crown by running a longer post-training pass on an unchanged base. Ultrafast took GPT-5.6 Sol to 14× real-time by rearchitecting inference on Cerebras silicon, without changing a weight. Qwen 3.8-27B distilled a 2.4-trillion-parameter teacher into 28 billion parameters that run on a single GPU. Three different labs, three different techniques, one shape: the base model is stable, and every serious capability move this week came from what you do after it — post-training, inference architecture, or distillation. Yesterday's signal said the frontier is moving into the layer around the model; today's says it is also moving into the layer between the model and the user. Base-model scale is not dead, but it is no longer the only lever, and this week the other levers moved further.
