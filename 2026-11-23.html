Lead Story

OpenRouter off-boards three AI hosts after researchers plant two million canary prompts — and show “Zero Data Retention” was not retained — OpenRouter & Dragos

On Saturday 22 November, inference router OpenRouter announced that it had off-boarded three upstream providers, stripped 125 endpoints from Zero-Data-Retention eligibility, and notified every potentially affected customer.[1] The trigger was a five-month experiment by HD Moore and his team at Dragos, published on 19 November, in which roughly two million prompts carrying unique canary tokens — AWS keys, trackable DNS names, one-shot URLs — were routed through live production inference endpoints and then watched for who called the bait.[2] Three providers showed evidence of data exposure. The earliest breach reached back to mid-July. On one reseller marketplace, sellers advertised popular models at the lowest price, quietly enabled request logging, and sold what passed through.[3]

The ugliest finding is not the thefts. It is the policy breach. One unidentified Chinese AI lab fetched the secret links from more than half of the prompts sent to its models between July and October — including about two-thirds of the prompts that had been explicitly tagged Zero Data Retention or no-data-collection.[2] On the public internet, the ZDR flag looked like a privacy guarantee. Inside the supply chain, it was closer to a sticker. Phala, Together AI and Novita have each already published their own incident notices confirming the affected windows; a fourth provider, Atlas Cloud, is named in OpenRouter's post.[1][4]

The architectural lesson is more important than the four names. The modern AI stack is a chain: a developer calls a gateway, the gateway routes to a provider, the provider sometimes resells capacity from a sub-provider, and each handoff has its own logging posture, its own key rotation, its own employee with shell access. Dragos's work shows that contract flags do not survive that many hops. The Hacker News framed this bluntly as a “prompt theft” story;[3] the more useful framing for builders is that inference is now a supply-chain-security problem, and “ZDR” is a claim that needs active verification — canary tokens, path probing, or vetted-provider allowlists — not a checkbox in an admin UI.

So what: treat every third-party inference endpoint your product calls as a potential logger until you have evidence otherwise — seed your own cheap canary prompts on each route this week, keep a short allowlist of providers who publish incident disclosures of this shape, and if a regulated workload is sitting on a “ZDR” flag alone, move it behind a first-party key before your next SOC-2 cycle.

The Briefs

US Commerce AI-chip “remote access” rule took effect last Thursday, requiring overseas data centres to license their largest tenants — Lexology & Ashurst / Perkins Coie

BIS's interim final rule amending the Export Administration Regulations came into force on 19 November. The new EAR Section 744.24 requires a US licence when an exporter has knowledge (or reason to know) that advanced AI chips will reach any data centre — wherever located — providing direct or indirect access of more than 12 million aggregate Total Processing Performance. Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil are named as new covered destinations; non-US providers that were already supplying restricted entities have 90 days to come forward for a licence.[5][6]

So what: if your product orchestrates inference across a multi-region cloud footprint that touches Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand or Brazil on non-US-subsidiary capacity, your procurement team has until mid-February to either confirm a licence path or re-route — “we thought it only applied to China” is not a defence under the new 12M-TPP threshold.

Nvidia confirmed on last week's earnings call that it has stopped shipping licensed Hopper-generation chips to China — Business Day / Gulf Business

On Tuesday 17 November's FY2026 quarterly call, interim CEO Colette Kress said Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper-generation data-centre product to China in the quarter ending 25 October, but has no current plans for further shipments.[7][8] Reporting on the sidelines put roughly 600,000 H200 chips in Hong Kong awaiting mainland approval that never arrived. The halt is being read as Nvidia pre-empting further BIS action rather than complying with one; the company did not commit to resuming shipments under any scenario.

So what: if your capacity plan for 2027 assumes Chinese demand continues to pull hardware away from the rest of the market, re-price it — the same silicon is now looking for a Western data-centre home, and Hopper-class capacity on non-Chinese clouds should get slightly cheaper or easier to book over the next two quarters.

Replit shipped Missions last week — agent-swarm work priced as an outcome and a spending cap, not as a seat — Replit

Replit's Missions, launched 17 November, lets a buyer set a goal, a hard budget, and an approvals list, and then runs a swarm of agents that plan, build, test and self-grade against success criteria. On a 13-task NumPy benchmark, Replit reported a 32-agent swarm hitting 19x the single-agent speedup;[9] internally, the team used Missions to build DataCat, an application-monitoring platform shipped in two days for about $3,000 in agent spend, now open-sourced.[9] The pricing model — dollars-per-mission, not seats-per-month — is the structural move; the benchmark numbers are secondary.

So what: if you sell agent work by the seat, the business you are competing with is now priced by the outcome and auditable against a budget line — rehearse a procurement conversation where the buyer's finance team asks for a dollar cap per deliverable and a receipt, because by Q2 at least one of your enterprise RFPs will.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Sales with 30+ partner plugins and persistent “dots” agents for deal monitoring — OpenAI

Also on 17 November, OpenAI released ChatGPT for Sales, bundling account research, meeting prep, CRM write-back and follow-up into a single surface, with first-party plugins for Salesforce, HubSpot, Gong, Clay and Outreach among more than thirty launch partners. The “dots” agents — persistent, between-conversation monitors — watch connected tools and surface prepared next steps the moment a seller returns.[10] The Sales plugin ships across all ChatGPT tiers, including Free, which puts the commodity version of sales-research AI on the desk of every incumbent vendor's customer base.

So what: if your product sells into SDR or AE teams on “we research prospects automatically,” your demo is now competing with a free plugin inside the buyer's chat window — retool the pitch around the integration or the compliance posture, because the research itself is no longer a wedge.

National Governors Association released a shared-principles framework for state AI regulation on Saturday — NGA (via PYMNTS)

On 22 November the National Governors Association — led by chair Wes Moore of Maryland and vice-chair Mike Braun of Indiana — released a set of principles for state AI regulation, timed to the new Congress convening in January. The framework names innovation-and-safety balance, human control, worker-and-consumer protection, and fair competition without favouring large incumbents; New York and California are both cited as live enforcement bellwethers.[11] It is a bipartisan signal that governors do not expect federal preemption, and that the fifty-state patchwork will be the operating environment for AI product teams through at least 2027.

So what: if your compliance posture still assumes a single federal AI regime is coming to supersede California and New York, drop that assumption — the governors have now said out loud that they will not wait, which means the 2027 deployment surface you need to design for is fifty jurisdictions concurrent, not one federal one.

Model Watch

GPT-6.2 Astra & Sol — OpenAI — 17 November

Astra is the first externally released OpenAI model to meet the lab's own “High” threshold for AI self-improvement — defined as “equivalent to giving every OpenAI researcher an additional, highly capable research engineer.”[12] Astra: $8 input / $0.40 cached / $40 output per million, a 20% cut on inputs and outputs and a 60% cached-input cut. Sol holds the GPT-6.1 Sol rates at $2 / $0.10 / $10. Context window is not named in the post. Both are available in ChatGPT Work, Codex, and the API; both ship an Ultrafast mode. Classifications are self-reported.

So what: the cached-input price cut is the actionable half — if your Astra workload has any stable preamble (system prompt, retrieved corpus, instruction stack), the caching discount reshapes unit economics faster than any benchmark on the page; the “High self-improvement” label is for your legal team's file, not your roadmap.

Gemini 4 Neon — Google DeepMind — 18 November

Gemini 4 Neon, the middle tier of the Gemini 4 family, prices at $3 input / $0.15 cached input / $15 output per million, with a 1M-token context window.[13] Google reports it ranks #1 overall on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index at 58.6 and scores 65.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1; a SWE-sweep mini-harness score of 39.0% inside Google Antigravity is called “state-of-the-art.” Four thinking levels (including xhigh) let callers trade latency against intelligence per request. Available in the Gemini API, Antigravity, the Gemini app for paid tiers, and the Fairwind Program for cybersecurity partners.

So what: if you run coding agents in production, Neon's price-per-task on Terminal-Bench puts the whole Gemini-4 family inside the band where you can justify a one-week shadow eval on your real traffic — don't migrate on the leaderboard, migrate on the eval.

Claude Fable 5.2 — Anthropic — 9 November (still the freshest Anthropic release)

Fable 5.2, released 9 November and now the default general-availability Anthropic model, lists at $8 / $40 per million input / output (20% below Fable 5.1), with a 1M context window, 128K maximum output, and an optional fast mode at $16 / $80. Anthropic reports 30% faster standard output generation vs 5.1.[14] Available across the Claude apps, Claude Platform, Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry; API model ID claude-fable-5-2. No new model has shipped from Anthropic in the past 48 hours; the week's Anthropic news was the IPO, not a release.

So what: re-run your Anthropic unit-cost math against the new standard rate — the 20% input-and-output cut combined with the fast-mode escape valve is the first time you can hold the same budget line while enabling fast mode on your latency-sensitive paths.

Voices

This edition's Voice is Yann LeCun, Chief AI Scientist at Meta and now Executive Chairman of the newly spun-out Advanced Machine Intelligence Labs. At Semafor's Silicon Valley & The World event in San Francisco on Tuesday 17 November, interviewed by Shelly Banjo, LeCun argued that the current moment where AI favours cyberattackers is “just a short period of transition”[15] — after which software will be, in his words, “hardened” by AI into an era of “unprecedented safety of software systems.”[15] It is a position worth sitting with for a week where the OpenRouter disclosures suggested the opposite, where Anthropic's Amodei debuted on Nasdaq arguing for pacing, and where LeCun himself has spent this weekend on X accusing Amodei of hyping danger to outlaw open models.[16] Two of the three most prominent voices in US frontier-lab discourse now hold directly opposing public positions on whether defence or offence wins the next twelve months — and unlike a year ago, both positions are on the record under a journalist's byline, not a tweet. The Semafor segment is the one to watch; LeCun's cyberattack passage lands early in the interview.

So what: when your security leadership asks whether to budget for AI-enabled offence or AI-enabled defence in the 2027 plan, the honest answer this month is that two credible lab principals are publicly disagreeing — so hedge both sides of the budget rather than citing one side as consensus, and revisit when a third serious voice stakes a position.

Today's Signal

The OpenRouter and Dragos disclosures reframed inference. “Zero Data Retention” turned out to be a contract claim, not a measurable property — the kind of promise that cannot survive a supply chain of resellers, sub-tenants, and foreign labs quietly following the breadcrumbs. The visible next move is the one builders should be planning for: a shift from flag-based trust to active monitoring, where the question your buyer asks stops being “does the vendor say ZDR?” and starts being “can the vendor show the canary logs?” This is the week the inference supply chain became a thing you have to audit.
