Lead Story — Anthropic Files for the Largest AI IPO Yet

The biggest financial event in AI's short history is now in motion. Anthropic has confidentially filed an S-1 with the SEC, prepping a Nasdaq listing targeted for October that bankers expect to raise more than $60 billion — what would be one of the largest technology offerings ever brought to market. The filing follows a Series H round that closed just before submission and valued the company at roughly $965 billion, edging past rival OpenAI and placing Anthropic within arm's reach of the trillion-dollar club before it has sold a single public share.

The numbers underneath the headline are what make builders sit up. Anthropic reported annualized revenue of about $30 billion as of April, up steeply from a year prior, with more than 500 enterprise customers each worth over $1 million annually and eight of the Fortune 10 among its top accounts. Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Morgan Stanley are leading the offering. Because the filing is confidential, the full S-1 — with its revenue breakdowns, customer concentration, and compute commitments — stays shielded from competitors on EDGAR until closer to the roadshow.

That secrecy is doing real work. The confidential route lets Anthropic test investor appetite and refine disclosures without handing OpenAI, Google, and xAI a quarter-by-quarter map of its economics. The open question is gross margin: training and serving frontier models is brutally capital-intensive, and the prospectus will eventually have to show how much of that $30 billion in revenue survives the cost of compute. Anthropic's earlier reported deal to buy up to a million Google TPUs suggests the infrastructure bill is enormous and growing.

Why it matters for builders: An Anthropic IPO hard-codes a public-market incentive into the model you may already be building on. Public companies optimize for predictable revenue and defensible margins, which tends to mean firmer enterprise pricing, clearer deprecation timelines, and heavier investment in the products that pay — agents, coding, and enterprise deployment — over pure research novelty. If you depend on a single provider's API, this is the moment to confirm your abstraction layer can survive a pricing change you don't control.
