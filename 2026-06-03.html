Lead Story

Washington wants a 30-day look at frontier models — and is asking nicely

President Trump on June 2 signed an executive order asking America's leading AI labs to voluntarily hand their most powerful new models to the federal government for testing up to 30 days before public release. The order leans on cooperation from the usual names — Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google — rather than compulsion, and it pointedly bars the government from standing up any mandatory licensing or pre-clearance regime. In other words: a request, not a rule.

The framing matters because the details have been a moving target. An earlier draft floated a 90-day review window; the final text cut that to 30. The White House had also been expected to sign weeks ago, then scrapped the plans amid worry that the order would slow American innovation while China presses on the frontier. The version that emerged is the cautious compromise — security review without the hard edge of a permitting system.

Beyond early access, the order directs federal agencies to build benchmarks for assessing the cyber capabilities of AI models, to stand up an "AI cybersecurity clearinghouse" for sharing vulnerability information, and to harden the government's own defenses. That cyber emphasis is the part most likely to outlast the headlines. A shared clearinghouse for model-discovered vulnerabilities is the kind of plumbing that, if it actually gets built, reshapes how disclosure works across the whole industry.

For builders, the near-term impact is muted — this binds frontier labs shipping the largest models, not the team wiring an API into a side project. But the direction of travel is worth tracking. Voluntary today tends to become expected tomorrow, and the benchmarks agencies are now tasked with writing will eventually shape what "safe enough to ship" means for everyone downstream.

The tell here is the word "voluntary." This administration chose persuasion over a permitting regime precisely because it fears handing China a head start. That instinct — security review, but never at the cost of speed — is the governing logic of U.S. AI policy right now, and it explains why nearly every constraint arrives with an opt-out attached. The Briefs

Microsoft ships its own models at Build, loosening the OpenAI leash

At Build in San Francisco, Microsoft unveiled MAI-Code-1-Flash (its first coding model) and MAI-Thinking-1 (a reasoning model), pitching both on efficiency and cost. Paired with Project Polaris — slated to replace GPT-4 inside GitHub Copilot by August — it's the clearest signal yet that Microsoft wants to own the model layer, not just rent it.

Anthropic files confidentially for an IPO

Anthropic submitted a draft S-1 to the SEC on June 1, less than a week after a $65B Series H pushed its valuation to $965B. Revenue run-rate is reportedly $47B, up from $10B a year ago. Share count and price aren't set — but the company has now stepped out ahead of OpenAI, which is readying a filing of its own.

Nvidia pushes into the AI PC

Nvidia unveiled a powerful Windows laptop chip, planting a flag in next-gen consumer machines built around on-device AI. Jensen Huang's bet: own every layer of the stack, from the datacenter down to the notebook on your desk.

Agent Mode becomes the default in Office — and the framework goes open

Microsoft made Agent Mode the default across Word, Excel, and PowerPoint, and open-sourced the Windows Agent Framework 1.0 under MIT. Add Azure AI Foundry's new first-party support for Claude, DeepSeek, Llama, and Mistral, and the platform story is suddenly model-agnostic by design.
