Lead Story: Washington Drops the Biggest AI Bill Yet — and It Would Freeze the States

Late Thursday, Representatives Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Lori Trahan (D-MA) released a 269-page discussion draft of the Great American Artificial Intelligence Act — the most comprehensive federal AI framework Congress has ever put on the table. The reaction over the weekend has been loud, polarized, and worth your attention, because this bill would reshape the regulatory ground every AI product stands on.

The headline provision is a three-year preemption of state AI laws governing frontier model development. That freeze would catch California's AI bills and — most immediately — Colorado's Consumer Protections for Artificial Intelligence Act, the first comprehensive state AI law in the country, scheduled to take effect June 30, just 23 days from now. Colorado's law requires developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems to guard against algorithmic discrimination in employment, education, lending, healthcare, and housing. If the federal bill passes, those protections go on ice before they ever apply.

In exchange, the draft asks something of the biggest labs. Companies with more than $500 million in annual revenue would have to publish public Frontier AI Frameworks describing how they govern their most capable models, report critical safety incidents to the federal government, and open their cybersecurity mitigation plans to auditors. The bill also funds a $100-million-a-year Center for AI Standards and Innovation inside the Commerce Department and creates criminal penalties for using AI to impersonate government officials.

The political lines formed fast. Labor unions — the AFL-CIO, AFT, and the Association of Flight Attendants — issued a joint rejection calling the draft "a giveaway to the AI industry." Tech trade groups like ITI and NetChoice praised it. The White House had already signaled its position: a December executive order specifically targeted Colorado's law, and this draft is the legislative follow-through.

Analysis: This is a discussion draft, not an introduced bill — the legislative road is long. But the practical takeaway for builders is binary and near-term. Either this framework advances and state rules freeze for three years, or it stalls and Colorado's law takes effect in roughly three weeks, meaning anyone with users in Colorado needs a compliance posture now. The disclosure requirements only bite at $500M+ revenue, so solo builders and small shops are spared direct obligations either way. The thing to watch is the vague "general applicability" carve-out for state laws — it's loose enough to litigate for a decade, which means uncertainty, not clarity, may be the bill's real legacy.
