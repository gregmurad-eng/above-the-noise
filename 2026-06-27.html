Lead StoryThe AI Trade Cracks — Markets Reprice the Boom

The AI rally that carried markets for three years is having its hardest reckoning yet. The Nasdaq Composite closed out a fifth straight losing session as investors rotated out of the megacap technology names that have defined the bull run, and the pain rippled across the Pacific: South Korea's Kospi plunged again, with Samsung and SK Hynix — the memory suppliers underpinning every AI data center — dropping more than 8% and 9% respectively in a single session.

This is no longer a one-day wobble. Microsoft and Meta have both fallen into bear-market territory, down roughly a fifth from their peaks, while Amazon, Apple, Alphabet, Nvidia and Tesla have all slipped into correction, off at least 10% from recent highs. The trigger isn't a single headline so much as a change in mood. After years of valuations built on the promise of AI rather than the bottom-line profit growth that usually justifies a soaring stock, investors are finally asking the uncomfortable question out loud: when does the spending pay off?

And the spending is staggering. Combined 2026 capital expenditures across Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta have now blown past $452 billion — a number that increasingly alarms the institutional money that has to underwrite it. Layered on top are rising bond yields and renewed worry that the Federal Reserve could hike rates, a particular threat to a sector that lives on cheap capital. A wave of high-profile talent departures hasn't helped sentiment either: Google alone has watched Nobel laureate John Jumper leave for Anthropic and Gemini co-lead Noam Shazeer head to OpenAI, feeding a narrative that even the leaders are reshuffling.

Most analysts are still calling this a correction rather than a collapse — prices falling from a stretched level while the underlying businesses stay healthy. That distinction matters. The cloud revenue is real, the chips are still sold out, and inference demand keeps climbing. But the era of buying anything with "AI" stapled to it appears to be over, replaced by a harder market that wants to see the return on half a trillion dollars of compute.

The analysis: For a solo builder, a market repricing the hyperscalers is mostly weather, not climate — but the second-order effects are worth watching. If capex discipline tightens, expect more pressure on the free tiers and generous credits that indie developers have feasted on, and a sharper industry focus on inference cost per token. That's exactly why custom silicon (see below) suddenly matters to everyone, not just the giants. Cheaper inference is the bridge between an unsustainable capex line and a business model — and the projects that win the next year will be the ones that already assumed compute wasn't free.

Today's Signal

$452 billion is the number to watch — not the stock tickers. The market's anxiety isn't really about a bad week; it's about whether four companies spending more than half a trillion dollars a year on compute can show a return that justifies it. For builders, that pressure is a gift in disguise: every dollar of capex anxiety pushes the industry toward cheaper inference, custom silicon, and on-device models. The cost of running AI is about to fall faster than the cost of training it. Build like compute is getting cheap, because the people writing the checks are betting their companies on exactly that.
