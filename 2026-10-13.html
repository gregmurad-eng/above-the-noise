Lead Story

Ukraine takes out three of Yandex's six datacenters in four days — and two of the three supercomputers that train Russia's AI — The Register

Between the overnight of October 7–8 and the overnight of October 11–12, Ukrainian long-range drones struck three Yandex datacenters inside Russia: Sasovo (Ryazan), Kaluga, and Vladimir. Yandex — Russia's largest tech company and the search, cloud, maps, taxi and payments backbone of the Russian consumer internet — confirmed the Sasovo and Vladimir sites were taken completely offline, with the third operating in limited mode.[1] Al Jazeera, citing Yandex and Russian regional governors, reported fires at Sasovo and the full shutdown of the Vladimir facility.[2] The Moscow Times put the business impact plainly: Yandex Pay offline, Yandex Cloud degraded, network connectivity to roughly 30% of normal.[3]

What makes this an AI story and not just a war story: the sites that were hit host two of Yandex's three supercomputers used to train AI models, including Galushkin in Vladimir, among the most powerful in Russia.[4] Tom's Hardware adds a second-order observation that is worth sitting with — Russian disinformation bot traffic measurably dropped overnight after the strikes, because the LLM infrastructure that generates it was literally offline.[4] Ukraine's foreign minister framed this as retaliation for Russian strikes on Kyiv datacenters that cut internet for about 100,000 households.[2]

The analytical point for anyone running compute: AI datacenters are now, formally, legitimate military targets in a shooting war, and the first operator to lose multiple sites in a single week is the national-champion incumbent, not a startup. The specific physical dependencies that make a frontier training run economical — dense GPU clusters in one hall, custom power, in-building cooling, one grid connection — are the same dependencies that make a datacenter a crisply-defined kinetic target. Russian regulators and operators have spent 2026 debating a "sovereign AI stack"; the week of October 7 is the week they discovered the stack is only as sovereign as the perimeter around it. The Record notes that even partially-restored Yandex services depend on the two remaining supercomputers running at elevated load, which is a reliability single-point-of-failure dressed as a redundancy plan.[5] No Western frontier lab has yet announced a response. Expect that to change in weeks, not months, because insurers and government customers will not wait for a US- or EU-sited rerun of the Vladimir incident to ask the question.

So what: if your training or inference footprint sits in a single building, a single metro, or a single jurisdiction, write that down as an auditable risk this week — the Yandex strikes just converted "geographic redundancy" from a FinOps slide into a line item underwriters will price, and the first vendor to publish a cross-region AI SLA with physical-infrastructure terms will set the market default.

Amazon is in talks to buy three-year-old Israeli AI startup Decart in a cash-plus-stock deal worth about $7 billion, up from Decart's May valuation of roughly $4 billion.[6] Decart's core products include software that abstracts over different AI chips (useful when you own as many Trainium parts as AWS does) and real-time 3D-world generation. Anthropic adds a "sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior" clause to its usage policy, effective November 12 — TechCrunch, The Register, CBS News

Anthropic updated its Usage Policy on October 8 to prohibit sustained, needless cruel behavior toward its Claude models, among other clarifications on election interference, surveillance, and physical-world agent control.[8] The Register and CBS confirm the rule applies only to extreme cases, keeps model-side conversation-ending as the primary enforcement mechanism, and takes effect November 12.[9][10] Builders should read it alongside the simultaneous clause on autonomous physical hardware: a qualified operator must be able to halt the equipment.

So what: if your product routes end-user chats to Claude, you now have 30 days to decide whether your TOS inherits Anthropic's model-welfare line or carves it out — the quiet default is that it inherits, and your legal team should know that before November 12, not on November 13.

Sakana AI releases MASS: one 27B model plays executor, workflow optimizer, and judge — and trains itself on its best team's runs — Sakana AI (primary)

Sakana AI and UC Berkeley released MASS (Multi-Agent Self-Supervision) on October 11, with the arXiv paper (2610.12176) and code on GitHub.[11] One Qwen3.6-27B instance is used three ways: optimizer, executor, and evaluator. Starting from the base model, MASS wins 53.9% of comparisons against itself after one round of self-training and 69.9% after two, judged by GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8. Score-per-output-token improves 1.2–1.6× across MLR-Bench, DSBench, ScienceAgentBench, and AstaBench.

So what: if your pipeline treats "the agent," "the planner," and "the critic" as separate model deployments, you're paying three times for architecture that MASS shows one 27B weight set can run — consolidate before your compute bill decides for you.

At its October 8 "Science: A New Golden Age" summit, the Trump administration announced $6B+ in science initiatives and $2.4B in compute credits from eleven companies to the DOE's Genesis Mission.[12][13] Pledges: NVIDIA $1B, AMD $500M, OpenAI $200M, Anthropic and Google $150M each, AWS/Armada/Crusoe/Micron $50M each. The centerpiece is DOE's Quantum Genesis Q Competition, funded at up to $215M for the first fault-tolerant 100-logical-qubit machines.

Microsoft-Decision-1 — Microsoft, October 9

Non-generative "decision model" that returns a probability over a fixed answer set (yes/no, multi-choice, rubric grade). MIT-licensed. $0.42/M input, output free. Microsoft claims 4.5× faster than Quyet-1.0-Large and 35× faster than GPT-6 Sol on routing-class tasks.[15]

So what: the routing/classification layer of your agent should move off a generative LLM this quarter — a free-output decision model at $0.42/M input is a step-change in cost basis for the "which tool do I call" subproblem.

Mistral Large 4 (Le Chonk) — public preview — Mistral AI, October 6

1.05T-parameter multimodal MoE, ~49B active, 1M context, API-only in preview with open weights promised by end of October. API $1.36/M in, $4.18/M out (temporary 50% sale: $0.68/$2.09). Primary release page: mistral.ai.[16]

So what: this is the first serious open-weight-bound frontier MoE from an EU lab in 2026 — if your deployment story involves "EU-sovereign weights on-prem by Q1," pencil in the end-of-October drop and start the eval today.

Claude Haiku 5.5 — Anthropic, October 7

Anthropic's first Haiku-class model with an adjustable effort setting (cost vs intelligence). ~75% cheaper to run on average than Haiku 4.5. $0.10/M input, $0.50/M output two-tier pricing. Model string: claude-haiku-5-5. Knowledge cutoff June 2026.[17]

Today's Signal

Four of today's five stories are about the same substrate: compute is no longer a layer under the AI stack, it is the asset. Ukraine took three Yandex datacenters offline including two supercomputers that train Russian LLMs. Amazon is in talks to pay $7B for a company whose core product is abstracting over which chip you call. The White House and eleven frontier players committed $2.4B in compute credits to a single federal research consortium. Mistral's Large 4 preview turns on API-only this week and open-weights by month's end — the differentiator is where the weights can physically live. The thread through all of it is that in October 2026 "compute" means a specific building, a specific jurisdiction, and a specific chip family, and treating it as fungible is now a materially mispriced position. Builders who priced their stack as "model + API" last quarter are being quietly repriced as "model + API + physical dependency," and the first vendors to publish that line item explicitly will take share from the ones who make customers calculate it themselves.
