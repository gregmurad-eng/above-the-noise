Lead Story

Apple bets the farm on Gemini — and rebuilds Siri from scratch

At WWDC 2026, the most independent company in tech quietly admitted it needed someone else's brain.

For three years the joke wrote itself: Siri, the assistant that launched the modern voice era, had become the one Apple users actively avoided. On Monday, Apple stopped laughing and did the thing it almost never does — it shipped someone else's model inside its own product. At WWDC 2026, the company unveiled a completely rebuilt Siri powered by a custom Google Gemini model, woven into the next generation of Apple Foundation Models behind Apple Intelligence.

The new Siri is not a patch. It launches as a standalone app that works across iPhone, iPad, Mac, and Vision Pro, opening with an "Ask Siri" bar that looks unmistakably like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini — type a question, attach a PDF or image with the paperclip, get a real answer. Apple is also adding "visual intelligence," letting Siri reason about whatever is on your screen or in front of your camera, plus a "Spatial Reframing" feature that uses 3D modeling to re-shoot an existing photo from a different angle. The assistant ships this fall to non-developers as a free software update.

Read past the demos and the strategic story is the interesting one. Apple has spent a decade selling on-device privacy and vertical integration as moral and technical superiority. Partnering with Google — its chief rival in mobile and the company whose search default funds a meaningful slice of Apple's services revenue — to supply the literal intelligence layer of its flagship assistant is a remarkable concession. It says Apple concluded that losing the assistant race outright was a bigger risk than the optics of renting a competitor's model. The carve-outs tell their own story: Siri's AI features won't launch in Europe or China, where regulatory and geopolitical friction made the Gemini arrangement untenable.

What it means for builders: the "build it all in-house" era of frontier AI is ending even at the company most associated with it. If Apple will white-label Gemini, the question for everyone smaller is no longer whether to depend on a frontier provider but how to architect around the one you choose — and how to stay portable when the relationship, the pricing, or the regulator changes. Watch closely how Apple exposes (or doesn't expose) this new Siri to third-party apps via App Intents; that surface is where the real platform opportunity for indie builders will live, far more than the keynote sizzle reel.
