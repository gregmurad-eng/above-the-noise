Lead Story

Six frontier CEOs signed a self-policing accord on superintelligence — and it is voluntary, two pages, and non-binding[1][2]

— The White House · via Al Jazeera, CNN

President Donald Trump gathered the heads of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Nvidia at the White House on September 29 and left with signatures on a document titled the "White House Accord on Super Intelligence: A Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities."[1] Dario Amodei signed for Anthropic, Greg Brockman for OpenAI, Sundar Pichai for Google, Mark Zuckerberg for Meta, Elon Musk for xAI, and Jensen Huang for Nvidia.[2]

The accord is two pages of principles. It asks each signatory to (1) run internal controls that monitor model capabilities during training and deployment across cybersecurity, biosecurity and chemical-threat surfaces; (2) maintain an internal team accountable for those controls; (3) partner with independent external auditors; and (4) meet regularly to set shared standards.[1] It carries no penalties, no timelines, and — crucially — no named auditors. Trump called it "morally binding" and, in the same appearance, an "AI constitution."[2]

The framing tells the story. The document uses "super intelligence" throughout — Trump signed a separate executive order the same day rebranding federal-agency AI language toward that term.[1] The tech chiefs were happy to sign, because "voluntary self-policing" is the pact that shuts down the possibility of a binding federal rule for the rest of this administration. Trump said the quiet part on the record: he wants "tremendous self-regulation" and views formal legislation as the wrong instrument.[2]

The genuinely testable pieces are two: an independent-auditor commitment and a regular-meeting cadence. A serious accord will name auditors and publish meeting readouts inside eight weeks. A performative one will produce neither. Everything else — safeguards, oversight teams, shared standards — is language every one of these six firms has already used in their own model cards for eighteen months.

If you build on frontier APIs, treat this as governance uncertainty risk falling in the near term and rising later — no binding federal rule is coming under this administration, but a future one may impose it retroactively on production systems, so keep your safety posture documented as if audits were live today.

— Reuters via CNBC, Fortune

Reuters reviewed the confidentially filed S-1: roughly $111.1B to Google, $110B to Amazon, $31.4B to Microsoft, $161.2B to Broadcom, and $84.5B to SpaceX in compute agreements.[5] Revenue grew 12x in 2025 to nearly $4.6B; net loss was $42B; the IPO could value the company above $2T.[6]
