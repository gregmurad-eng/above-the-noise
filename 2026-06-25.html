Lead Story

Google's Brain Drain Becomes a Stampede as DeepMind Stars Defect to Anthropic and OpenAI

The AI Talent War

The most valuable asset in artificial intelligence isn't a data center or a model checkpoint — it's the few hundred people who know how to build the next one. This week made that brutally clear for Google. Two more senior DeepMind researchers, Jonas Adler and Alexander Pritzel, are reportedly on their way to Anthropic, the latest defections in an exodus that has turned from a worrying trend into an open crisis.

Adler worked on Google's AI coding efforts; Pritzel focused on pretraining. Both contributed to the AlphaFold research that earned DeepMind's John Jumper a share of a Nobel Prize — and Jumper himself announced just days earlier that he, too, is leaving for Anthropic. Layer on Noam Shazeer, the Transformer co-inventor who helped lead Gemini before departing for OpenAI, and a pattern emerges that no amount of corporate messaging can spin away. The people who built Google's frontier are walking out the door, and they're walking toward its two fiercest rivals.

The gravity pulling them is financial as much as intellectual. Anthropic recently closed a round at a reported $965 billion valuation — surpassing OpenAI — and has confidentially filed for an IPO. OpenAI is preparing its own filing, with a listing possible as soon as September. For a researcher sitting on Google equity that's already fully liquid, joining a soon-to-be-public lab offers the rare prospect of a generational payday. Established tech salaries, however large, can't replicate pre-IPO upside.

But money isn't the whole story. Reporting points to internal friction over compute — Google reportedly reassigned capacity away from one of Shazeer's projects, a flashpoint as teams fight over a finite pool of advanced chips. When your best people feel starved of the very resource they need to do their best work, a competitor's open checkbook starts to look like more than a raise. It looks like respect.

For builders, the signal underneath the headlines is about momentum, not melodrama. Google still ships capable models, but Gemini 3.5 Flash and 3.1 Pro have slipped outside the top five on several leaderboards, trailing Anthropic, OpenAI, and even Chinese labs. Talent flight and benchmark slippage feed each other: researchers chase the labs that ship, and the labs that ship attract the researchers. If you're choosing a model stack or a platform to bet a product on, watch where the people go — it's a leading indicator that the benchmarks only confirm months later.

The Briefs

OpenAI ships GPT-5.5-Cyber — and locks it down

OpenAI released the full GPT-5.5-Cyber under its Daybreak security initiative, posting a record 85.6% on CyberGym (vs. 81.8% for standard GPT-5.5) and big jumps on ExploitGym and SEC-bench Pro. Notably, it's not a public API model — access is gated to vetted organizations through a Trusted Access program, a sign of how cautiously frontier labs now treat offensive-capable tooling.

OpenAI's first in-house inference chip, "Jalapeño"

OpenAI unveiled Jalapeño, its first Intelligence Processor — a custom inference accelerator co-designed with Broadcom, with early testing pointing to strong performance per watt. It's the clearest signal yet that the frontier labs intend to own their own silicon rather than rent it indefinitely.

Chinese labs keep squeezing Western pricing

MiniMax's M2.5 continues to set the cost-performance pace — strong coding scores (80.2% SWE-Bench Verified) at a claimed tenth of Western prices, roughly a dollar an hour of continuous operation. For solo builders, the open-weight Chinese tier is becoming a serious default for anything cost-sensitive.

The IPO race is reshaping the whole field

Anthropic's confidential IPO filing and OpenAI's imminent one aren't just finance news — they're the engine behind the talent war, the compute arms race, and the pricing pressure all at once. Public markets are about to get a direct read on frontier-AI economics.

Infrastructure becomes the kingmaker

Reflection AI is reportedly raising again at a $25 billion valuation, while SpaceX's compute business is quietly emerging as an infrastructure layer beneath several top labs. The lesson holds: in 2026, whoever controls compute controls the roadmap.
