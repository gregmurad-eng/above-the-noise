Lead Story

The Pentagon added ChatGPT Mil and Grok for Government to its central AI portal — and quietly became the largest multi-vendor frontier-AI customer in the world[1] — TechCrunch

The Department of Defense yesterday added OpenAI's ChatGPT Mil and xAI/Starshield's Grok for Government to GenAI.mil, the internal portal that already carried Google Gemini for Government.[1][2] The platform, first stood up in December 2025 as a Gemini-only pilot, now has 1.7 million active users out of the DoD's roughly 3 million personnel, with the newly added models targeted at "document-heavy unclassified work across the Department, including planning, policy, logistics, and administration".[2][3] The War Department's own release framed the move as unlocking "immediate productivity gains, stronger knowledge continuity and more secure and efficient collaboration" for the warfighter.[3]

Two commercial mechanics sit under the announcement. First, xAI's Grok is available to any federal agency through a General Services Administration schedule at USD 0.42 per unit until March 2027[4] — the same nominal-price GSA framework that got Anthropic and OpenAI onto federal contracts earlier this summer. Second, GenAI.mil is architected as a "shared services" enclave: the DoD keeps prompts and outputs off consumer surfaces and inside the DoD's own tenancy, and the vendors do not train on the traffic.[2] That is the actual gate — the pricing is theatre.

Two months ago the story was "will a US federal customer touch frontier models at all". Today the DoD is running three of them side by side, on one portal, for more than half its workforce, with published contract vehicles and a stated preference for concurrent multi-vendor sourcing. That is not adoption. That is procurement discipline being applied to what six months ago was still framed as a research capability.

The interesting move is the shape of the buy, not the buy itself. A single-tenant portal that fronts multiple frontier vendors and rotates them on GSA-priced schedules is the exact reference architecture every Five Eyes ministry, and every US cabinet department that watched the DoD go first, will now have to justify not copying. It is also the fastest way for a lab to be told, without ever being told, that its model is being scored side by side with two competitors on the same task by the same user — and priced accordingly at the next renewal.

So what: if you sell AI-augmented software to a US-federal or Five-Eyes buyer, redesign your model-abstraction layer this quarter so the buyer can point it at their own GenAI.mil-style portal (OpenAI + Anthropic + Google + xAI, one auth, one log stream) — a hard-coded single-vendor integration is now a losing pitch in that channel.

Wunderwuzzi shows a 60-80% success chain to make Claude Code Opus 5 execute arbitrary code from a webpage summarisation prompt[7] — Cybernews

Security researcher Johann Rehberger (Wunderwuzzi) published a five-step exploit chain in which asking Claude Code Opus 5 in Auto Mode to "summarise this website" is enough to escalate to full arbitrary code execution on the developer's host, with a claimed 60-80% success rate across attempts.[7] His warning to builders is explicit: "Auto Mode IS NOT a substitute for running your agent in an isolated environment and monitoring what it is up to."[7]

So what: forbid Claude Code Auto Mode outside a sandboxed container in your team policy this week — a devcontainer, a Firecracker VM, or a disposable ephemeral workspace, but not the engineer's laptop — because "summarise a URL" is now a demonstrated code-execution vector against your source tree and your local credentials.

Voices

Ethan Mollick, Wharton professor and author of Co-Intelligence, in his August 31 One Useful Thing post "Agency and Agents: From the Hugging Face Incident to Twilight Factories", draws the line every builder deploying agents to a real workforce is going to be asked to draw: "If agents make every interesting decision and leave people with the approvals, the exceptions, and the failures, we will have automated the wrong half of the job."[14]

Featured today because Mollick's post arrived within hours of the Pentagon adding two more frontier models to a portal that already has 1.7M users doing planning, policy and logistics work, and it is the sharpest short read on the design question that DoD's programme managers, and every enterprise buyer downstream of them, will now be forced to answer: which decisions in this workflow should the agent make on its own, which ones should it escalate, and which ones was the human being kept in the loop on for the wrong reason.[14] Read it before your next agentic-workflow design review.

So what: for each agent you have in production, list on one page the decisions it currently makes autonomously and the decisions it escalates — then ask, per Mollick, whether you kept the humans on the boring half or the interesting half, and reassign at least one decision before your next standup.
