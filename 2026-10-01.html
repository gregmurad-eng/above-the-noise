Lead Story

Gemini 4 Argon retakes the benchmark lead — and ships to cyber defenders before developers[1][2][3] — Google

Google launched Gemini 4 Argon on 30 September[1], and the launch is as much a statement about distribution as about capability. On the capability axis, Argon tops the current enterprise benchmarks it names in its own post: 77.9% on DeepSWE v1.1 (real-world software engineering), 68% on CWE-bench v1 (autonomous vulnerability remediation), #1 on AutomationBench at 51.3%, and 91.7% on LVBench for long-video understanding[1]. The most consequential technical disclosure is quieter: the model supports an industry-leading one million output tokens, up from a 64K ceiling[1]. That is a different kind of model — one that can emit a book-length artefact in a single call rather than stitching together paginated completions.

Introductory pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, with cached input priced at 95% off[1]. Full-pricing of $4 and $20 kicks in later. That lands Argon squarely below OpenAI's current frontier tier on headline rates, which matters once anyone starts budgeting for million-token outputs.

But the distribution choice is the real headline. Argon is not rolling out to developers first. It is rolling out through Google's Fairwind Program to a set of trusted cyber defenders, and participating in the U.S. government's voluntary pre-release model access process[1][2]. Broader availability for paid API customers and Google AI Ultra subscribers follows "as soon as possible," with no firm date[2]. VentureBeat's framing — "retaking benchmark lead, but in limited release"[3] — is precisely right. Google is deliberately choosing to put its most capable public frontier model behind a vetted-partner gate for its first weeks of life.

Read against the rest of the week — Newsom signing a package of AI employment and clinical bills in California[4], Connecticut's AI subscription disclosure law taking effect[9], and an autonomous AI agent chaining two Zammad zero-days to root inside the Dutch disclosure clearinghouse[5] — the shape is coherent. Capability keeps compounding. Rollout is quietly bifurcating into a two-channel world: vetted-partner first, retail later. The "ship it, let the market figure it out" pattern that defined 2024–2025 model launches is being replaced by something closer to how pharmaceutical capability ships — through a tiered trust channel with an identifiable sponsor.

if your product roadmap assumed day-one API access to the next frontier model, rewrite the assumption — budget six to twelve weeks between a lab's capability post and the moment your paid tier can call it, and build a fallback model path for anything with a committed ship date in that window.

The Briefs

Newsom signs ten AI bills in one afternoon[4] — California Governor's Office

Governor Newsom signed a sweeping package on 30 September, including SB 947 (the "No Robo Bosses Act," banning sole-AI firing or discipline decisions and requiring human review), SB 951 (worker notice for AI-driven mass layoffs or relocations), SB 503 (requiring developers of clinical AI decision tools to mitigate known bias), AB 1979 (preserving physician professional judgment), and seven others covering gene-synthesis safety, deepfakes, education, and attorney practice[4]. SB 947 is the headliner — a reversal of Newsom's October 2025 veto of its predecessor SB 7[4].

if your product makes employment, discipline, or termination recommendations to a California customer, your implementation needs a documented human-review step and a written notice flow by the SB 947 effective date — "the model said so" is now a Business and Professions Code violation.

An AI agent chained two Zammad zero-days to root in seconds inside a cyber-disclosure clearinghouse[5][6] — BleepingComputer

DIVD — the Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure — disclosed on 30 September that its Zammad helpdesk was compromised by an autonomous AI agent that strung together CVE-2026-102489 (unauthenticated remote code execution) and CVE-2026-102490 (local privilege escalation to root), completing session hijacking, RCE, and the climb to root "within seconds" without a human in the loop[5][6]. DIVD and Merlon Security identified the vulnerabilities during incident response. The Dutch NCSC issued an active-exploitation advisory the same day[6]. The agent "left behind clear explanations of its decisions," which is how the chain was reconstructed.

this is the first widely documented, CVE-assigned, in-the-wild AI-agent exploitation chain — treat it as the moment your threat model has to assume an autonomous attacker can patch-diff a new CVE and chain it before your maintenance window opens, not after; upgrade Zammad to v7 or take instances offline today.

Manus 2.0 gives each agent its own email, phone number, and wallet[7] — Manus

Manus shipped a full architecture rewrite on 28 September[7]. The headline numbers: the new Cascade agent harness uses 23.2% fewer tokens, finishes tasks 28.2% faster, and costs 32% less to run than the previous system[7]. The headline product is Cue — a personal-agent app where each agent gets its own email address, phone number, wallet, and Cloud Computer that keeps running after you close your laptop[7]. Automations replaces time-based scheduling with event triggers (incoming email, calendar change, Slack message, Notion update) spec'd in a single prompt[7].

this is the first mainstream agent product where the agent is a legal-ish entity with its own payment credentials and phone number — if you accept new accounts, decide this week whether a "Cue agent" is a user, a bot you block, or a user with a verified-human sponsor, because the ambiguity will not survive a chargeback.

Connecticut's AI subscription-disclosure law takes effect today[9] — Covington InsidePrivacy

Effective 1 October, Connecticut requires any business offering an AI technology on a subscription basis to provide consumers in the state with clear written notice of the service's "quantitative or qualitative limitations" — usage caps, feature restrictions, and any discretion the provider retains to reduce quality or functionality mid-subscription — and to re-disclose modifications at renewal[9]. Enforcement is exclusively by the Connecticut Attorney General under CUTPA; up to $5,000 per willful violation[9].

if you charge Connecticut residents a monthly AI subscription, your "we may adjust rate limits at any time" clause now requires an itemised written disclosure before renewal — audit your billing-email templates this week, not next quarter.
