Lead Story

OpenAI says an internal model plus 10,000 agents produced a Lean-verified proof of finite-time blow-up for Navier–Stokes — and the announcement is now overshadowed by a credit dispute with the NYU mathematician who says he was there first — OpenAI[1]

OpenAI announced on Tuesday 8 September that an unreleased internal model — described as significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra — plus roughly 10,000 coordinated agents produced, over 88 hours starting 28 August, a proof that solutions to the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time[1][2]. The write-up ships with a Lean formalisation and answers a Millennium Prize question that the Clay Mathematics Institute has carried $1M against since 2000[3].

The result is real, and the technical arc is genuinely new: agent-swarm mathematics with formal verification, not a chatbot writing a proof. Semafor and Quanta Magazine both talked to independent experts who read the Lean file[2][3]. But the announcement landed inside a public credit fight, and that is the story that will actually decide how the next agent-generated proof gets received.

NYU Courant's Tristan Buckmaster — Clay Research Award winner on adjacent Euler blow-up work — told Axios and Fortune that he and Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge had spent nearly a year using AI to chase fluid blow-up, and reached a solution around 22 August[4][5]. He alleges OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck learned about the unpublished work, then in a phone call proposed either consecutive-day releases with OpenAI going second, or that Buckmaster publish alone with an OpenAI internal-model credit line and Alpöge dropped from authorship because Alpöge's employment at Anthropic was "annoying"[5]. When Buckmaster threatened to go public, he says Bubeck warned "Why would you ruin your career?" and "If you don't want me to be nice, then I don't have to be nice"[5].

Bubeck's response on X is now the counter-record: "A series of false and inflammatory allegations against me are currently circulating on social channels"[6]. He denies model contamination — "we did not use their prompts or proofs to prompt our models or direct our agents"[6] — posts what he says are texts showing he offered the Buckmaster/Alpöge team the option to publish first, and apologises for the "ruin your career" line specifically as "the opposite of what I was trying to convey"[6]. Both accounts are on the record. Both cannot be right.

Why this is the lead and not a footnote: Terence Tao — Fields Medalist, currently the loudest voice in the room on AI-in-mathematics — posted on Mathstodon on 9 September that "even the rumor of someone working on a problem can trigger a massive amount of AI-powered effort to flatten it before the original research project has time to reach its full potential," warning that this "would reverse centuries of traditions of open science and do serious long-term damage to the future of the field"[7]. The Buckmaster–Bubeck dispute is Tao's abstract concern rendered in the concrete: the first big-name AI-agent proof produces a credit fight before the ink is dry, and now every mathematician deciding whether to share a promising direction on a preprint server has a fresh reason to keep quiet.

So what: if your product runs agent swarms against public problems — bug bounties, exploit discovery, dataset cleaning, model-eval design — assume you now need a written "who worked on this and when" disclosure protocol before the run starts, not after. The rules mathematicians are about to write for AI-in-math are the template every other agent-competitive field will copy, and being first with a defensible provenance record is cheaper than being second with a lawyer.

Google DeepMind ships AlphaGenome Atlas — precomputed molecular predictions for all 9 billion possible single-letter DNA variants in the human genome, plus a variant-ranking score — Google DeepMind[10]

Announced 8 September, Atlas is roughly one petabyte and more than 30× the size of the AlphaFold Database DeepMind expanded in 2022[10][11].
