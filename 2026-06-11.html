Lead Story

Oracle's plunge is the market finally pricing the AI capital bill

Oracle shares fell close to 9% as investors digested a number that no longer fit the spreadsheet: the company guided fiscal 2027 capital spending well above Wall Street estimates and said it would raise nearly $40 billion through a mix of debt and equity to keep building. For a stock that spent the past year as a darling of the AI-infrastructure trade, the reaction marked a turn — the market is no longer rewarding ambition for its own sake; it wants to see the cash come back out the other side.

The fundamentals underneath are staggering in both directions. Oracle is sitting on a backlog of remaining performance obligations north of $550 billion, the strongest evidence yet that demand for AI compute is real and contracted, not speculative. But servicing that backlog means roughly $50 billion of capex this fiscal year alone, plus more than $260 billion in additional data-center lease commitments stretching out over the coming years. Free cash flow has gone negative. One analyst, downgrading the stock to Hold, described the company bluntly as having transformed from a software vendor into an AI capacity-financing operation.

That phrase is the whole story. The hyperscaler land grab has quietly mutated into a financing race. Morgan Stanley now expects AI-related global debt issuance to more than double to nearly $570 billion this year, as the biggest players reach past their own balance sheets and equity markets to fund the buildout. OpenAI is chasing 10 gigawatts of capacity; China has floated a $295 billion national data-center plan. The constraint on frontier AI has moved up the stack — from talent, to chips, to power, and now to the cost of capital itself.

What it means for builders: none of this changes your weekend sprint, but it shapes the ground you're building on. The compute you rent is being financed on debt that assumes years of strong demand. That's bullish for capacity and probably bearish for the steep price cuts we've grown used to — the providers carrying this debt need margin. If you're architecting anything that leans on a single provider's cheap inference staying cheap, build the abstraction layer now. The era of compute being priced like a loss-leader is ending, and the bond market just sent the memo. (Yahoo Finance)

OpenAI has reportedly surpassed $25 billion in annualized revenue and is taking early steps toward a public listing, potentially as soon as late 2026. A debut at that scale would be the clearest signal yet that the generative-AI business has graduated from venture story to public-market reality. (Tech Startups)

Microsoft ships seven in-house MAI models

Microsoft unveiled a family of seven models built in-house, led by MAI-Thinking-1, a mid-sized reasoning model that matches frontier systems on software-engineering benchmarks, and MAI-Code-1-Flash, a 5B-active-parameter agentic coder tuned for GitHub Copilot and VS Code. The strategy: lessen reliance on OpenAI and cut costs for developers on the Microsoft stack. (CNBC)

A cooling startup borrows from nuclear reactors

Ferveret, founded by MIT researchers, is developing a chip-cooling system inspired by nuclear-reactor thermal management, aiming to slash the energy and water that AI data centers burn keeping silicon cold. As power and water become the binding constraints on the buildout, cooling is quietly becoming a frontier of its own. (MIT News)

Study: AI may dull our fake-news radar

An MIT Media Lab study finds that leaning on AI to vet information can erode our own ability to spot falsehoods — much as GPS atrophied our sense of direction. A useful caution for anyone wiring an LLM into a product as the arbiter of what's true. (MIT News)

Google brings Gemini to the 2026 World Cup

Google is folding Gemini into the 2026 FIFA World Cup through a partnership with Argentina's national team and a slate of AI-powered fan features — a sign of how aggressively the big labs are pushing assistants into mainstream consumer moments. (Tech Startups)
