Lead Story

The AI giants are going public — and Washington wants a cut

Two storylines that defined the weekend point at the same question: who will own the AI boom? OpenAI is working with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley on a confidential IPO that could land as soon as September, at a private-market valuation now pegged between roughly $730 billion and $850 billion — a listing that would rank among the largest in history. The company reportedly filed its confidential S-1 with the SEC in late May, just days after rival Anthropic submitted its own draft registration. The back half of 2026 is shaping up as the first real test of whether public investors will pay the prices private markets have set for AI. (CNBC, Tech Times)

At the same moment, President Trump told reporters Friday that the federal government may take direct equity stakes in OpenAI, xAI, and other leading labs — "You make them a partnership in this revolution." The structure under discussion is unusual: rather than the government buying in with cash, OpenAI would donate equity to seed a so-called "Public Wealth Fund," a sovereign vehicle Sam Altman first floated to the administration in 2025 and revived in an April policy proposal. No taxpayer outlay, but a permanent public claim on the upside. (Fortune, MLQ News)

What makes the moment strange is the politics. From the left, Senator Bernie Sanders is pushing further — calling for 50% government equity stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI plus a 50% tax on their stock, effectively partial nationalization. A populist right and a democratic-socialist left have, for different reasons, arrived at the same instinct: these companies are too consequential to be purely private. Anthropic, for its part, has publicly confirmed it is not involved in the equity-stake discussions.

For everyone else, the takeaway is that the AI labs are crossing from startup mythology into the machinery of public capital and public politics — quarterly earnings, lockup periods, and now the question of whether the state holds a seat on the cap table.

Analysis: An IPO and a government stake pull in opposite directions, and the tension is the story. Public markets demand legible margins and predictable growth; a state equity holder demands strategic control and a slice of the windfall. A company trying to satisfy both at once may find its hands tied right when it most needs to move fast. For builders, watch the second-order effect: once labs are publicly traded and partly state-aligned, their API pricing, model-release cadence, and willingness to ship frontier capabilities all become subject to forces that have nothing to do with the technology. Plan your dependencies as if your model vendor will get slower and more cautious, not faster and bolder, over the next two years.

The Briefs

ModelsMicrosoft ships its own models to cut the OpenAI cord

At Build, Microsoft unveiled MAI-Thinking-1, its first in-house reasoning model — a 35B-active-parameter MoE with a 256K context window, trained entirely on licensed data with no distillation from third parties. Microsoft claims it matches Claude Opus 4.6 on SWE-Bench Pro. Alongside it, the 5B MAI-Code-1-Flash is now live inside GitHub Copilot and VS Code, pitched as a low-latency, low-cost coding workhorse. The strategic message is louder than the benchmarks: Microsoft wants to lessen its reliance on OpenAI. (CNBC, Microsoft AI)

Company MovesApple plays Switzerland with a $1B licensing bet

Apple reportedly signed a roughly $1B/year AI licensing deal while keeping its own engineering focused on private compute and on-device hardware integration — a posture that could make it the neutral party in the AI wars rather than a combatant. The bet: own the distribution layer and the privacy story, and let others bleed cash training frontier models. (BuildFastWithAI)

HealthcareAI reads the retina where specialists are scarce

French startup Zenkolab is using AI to analyze retinal images and flag eye diseases early, targeting regions with few eye-care specialists and aiming to cut diagnostic delays. It's a reminder that beyond the model-release horse race, the durable value of this technology is often quietest: triage, screening, and access in places that never had it. (AI News Weekly)

RoboticsRobotaxis, humanoids, and digital IDs land in the same week

The embodiment wave kept rolling: Uber put robotaxis on the road in Spain, BYD signaled an entry into humanoid robotics, and Google Wallet expanded into digital identity. Each is small on its own; together they sketch a world where AI stops living behind a chat box and starts moving through physical and civic infrastructure. (BuildFastWithAI)

Today's Signal

The week's headlines aren't really about IPOs or new models — they're about AI graduating from a product story into an institutional one. Capital markets, government equity, national strategy, physical robots on public roads. If you're a solo builder, that shift is your moat: the giants are about to get slower, more political, and more accountable to shareholders. Speed, taste, and proximity to a real user's problem are things no public wealth fund can put on a balance sheet.
