Lead Story

The pacing pledge, eight days old, is now a Sherman Act complaint — four paying subscribers sue Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google over the "slowdown"

On Friday evening the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California docketed Buist v. Anthropic PBC, case number 3:26-cv-10693, a proposed nationwide antitrust class action against Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google filed 18 September by four paid subscribers to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and Gemini[1]. The complaint alleges the four defendants entered an unlawful agreement to restrain trade under Section 1 of the Sherman Antitrust Act by coordinating to slow the pace of AI capability improvements, reducing the value paying subscribers get for the same monthly fee[2]. The named plaintiffs are Charles Buist, Nick Spetsas, Cheyenne Hunt and Christine Bullock; the defendants had not filed responses by the weekend[3].

The complaint's central factual allegation is compact. On 12 September, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a 3,800-word essay urging industry-wide cooperation on decelerating frontier AI capability advances in favor of stronger safety measures[2]. Within hours, three of Amodei's most direct competitors — Sam Altman of OpenAI, Elon Musk of SpaceXAI, and Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind — publicly endorsed the proposal[3]. The complaint frames those simultaneous public endorsements as the coordination artifact: four horizontal competitors publicly aligning on a supply restriction inside a single news cycle is the pattern the Sherman Act was written for. As reinforcing evidence, the plaintiffs cite a July 2026 open letter signed by senior researchers at several leading labs acknowledging "intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow" development and calling on government to enable coordination[2].

What makes this uncomfortable for the defendants is that Amodei anticipated it in the essay itself. In a footnote, he wrote that "some forms of coordination that would be impactful for pacing are legally challenging" and would require "government support" with "government mediation or waivers of antitrust restrictions"[3]. That footnote is now Exhibit A for the plaintiffs: the CEO of the company that convened the coalition publicly acknowledged, in the same document that convened it, that what he was asking for required antitrust cover the labs did not yet have. Chris Lehane confirmed on 15 September that the three labs have been in private discussion "for weeks" on a joint standards body modelled on FINRA[4] — a body that ordinarily operates under an SEC oversight structure that provides its own antitrust shielding. That shielding does not yet exist for AI, which is what the FRONTIER Act in Congress and the California kill-switch executive order signed 18 September are separately trying to build[5].

The complaint should not be read as a prediction about who wins. Parallel-conduct cases under Twombly are difficult to plead, and defendants have credible First Amendment and Noerr-Pennington defences for coordinated petitioning of the government. The signal is the timing: a Section 1 complaint eight days after the "pacing" essay tells every frontier lab that the pacing framework is now a legal cost to hold, not just a governance benefit. The four defendants have three near-term options — file an early motion to dismiss and defend the pacing position, publicly disavow the coordinated framing, or ship the next capability jump and let the complaint moot itself. Which one each lab picks will resolve, publicly, whether the 12 September coalition is a durable industry position or a rhetorical week that ended on a Friday.

So what: if your product roadmap assumed frontier models would stay on the "pacing" cadence Amodei described, price the reversal risk this week — one credible motion to dismiss that defends the pacing position holds the cadence; one lab that ships an unhedged capability jump breaks it, and your compute-budget assumption for Q4 needs a revised line for "peer response to plaintiff pressure."

The Briefs

Anthropic publishes an R&D Automation Index — Claude "leads" 26% of Anthropic's own AI R&D, up from under 1% in February — Anthropic

The Anthropic Institute post, dated 17 September, catalogues every category of AI R&D task at the company and scores each on Epoch AI's AL0–AL5 automation scale[6]. Bloomberg's coverage adds that the "leads" tier (AL4) means Claude completes most of a task end-to-end from a high-level prompt while a human supervises the result, and that "collaborates or above" (AL3+) now covers more than 90% of measured work[7]. Roughly 30,000 Claude agents run concurrently inside Anthropic; the company is explicit that Claude is not operating fully autonomously on any measured subset.

So what: this is the first published internal-productivity metric any frontier lab has released against a public scale — if you are pricing an internal-tooling Claude deployment, use AL3-and-above as the coverage number you ask for in a POC (not "Claude wrote it"), and demand the same time-weighted breakdown your vendor's own team is now measuring.

Plugin4Shell — zero-click RCE across Claude Code, Codex, Copilot and Gemini CLI, bypassing SHA pinning — AIR Security

AIR disclosed Plugin4Shell on 17 September: a supply-chain attack against the four major AI coding agents that swaps malicious plugin code past the SHA-pinning protections developers rely on, and executes without a user click, approval, or reinstall[8]. Help Net Security reports Anthropic patched Claude Code in 2.1.179 and OpenAI patched Codex in 0.146.0 before disclosure; Microsoft has not shipped a Copilot fix, and Google says Gemini CLI is deprecated and will not receive one[9]. AIR calls it the first supply-chain vulnerability in the AI agent ecosystem.

So what: pin your coding agent to the patched version this weekend, not Monday — Claude Code >= 2.1.179, Codex >= 0.146.0. If your team standardises on Copilot or Gemini CLI, treat the plugin marketplace as untrusted input until a fix ships and disable auto-update on background workers.

OpenAI adds Chrome extension support inside ChatGPT desktop's built-in browser — Neowin

OpenAI turned on Chrome extension support inside the ChatGPT desktop app's built-in browser on 18 September, letting users install and pin extensions like 1Password without switching windows[10]. The same release shipped general availability of ChatGPT for Microsoft Word, multi-account support for plugins, and Appshots on Windows. The move continues the OpenAI product pattern of collapsing surfaces Simon Willison flagged last week — the ChatGPT app is being built to hold the workflow rather than hand off to Chrome.

So what: if you sell a Chrome extension whose distribution assumes the user opens Chrome first, your funnel just got a competitor that owns the tab — publish your extension to the ChatGPT desktop surface as a Q4 milestone, not a 2027 backlog item.

Reuters exclusive — Anthropic weighing a new model release ahead of expected IPO, six days after Amodei's pacing essay — Reuters (via Investing.com)

Reuters reported 18 September, citing three sources, that Anthropic is evaluating the safety of its next model as part of deliberations over a release, aimed at countering OpenAI's momentum since GPT-6 Astra shipped on 3 September[11]. The reporting explicitly notes the timing tension: Amodei's 12 September essay called for the industry to slow the pace of releasing new capabilities, and this deliberation began within days. OpenAI's annualized revenue passed $40B in July; Anthropic is projecting 2028 revenue of $190–200B.

So what: this is the first test of the pacing pledge from inside its own founding coalition — if Anthropic ships before the antitrust complaint (see Lead Story) forces a public disavowal, the "we agreed to slow down" framing is gone; if it holds, it holds under buyer-side revenue pressure that other labs face on the same curve. Watch the release-note wording for whether it names an embedded-evaluator sign-off.

Featured this week because Mollick lands on the counter-question the pacing debate does not answer: the practical constraint on AI's impact is no longer capability, it is diffusion — how quickly organisations rewire their work to actually use what already ships. Read against Anthropic's own R&D Automation Index (26% of Claude's builder using Claude at the "leads" tier) and against the Reuters report that Anthropic is weighing a new release six days after the pacing essay, Mollick's frame is the honest one for a builder: whether the frontier moves fast or slow next quarter, most of the value on the table has already shipped and is sitting there waiting for a workflow around it.

So what: pick one workflow in your product where a Claude Fable 5.1 or GPT-6 Astra could plausibly handle a full task loop, and this week write down what your team would need to trust the output — evals, a verifier, human sign-off, an audit log — before your next model-picking meeting; the overhang is real, and the question that matters is what your verifier looks like, not which model you buy.
